what is time?

The universe is old: 13.77 billion years ± 59 million. For aeons time passed without anyone noticing, and then mankind started tracking time, which they did by observing the movements of the heavenly bodies, the stars, the sun, the moon.

Early humans saw the cycles of natures, and used them to track time. These natural cycles became the foundations of how human cultures measured the passing of time. Awkwardly these cycles do not line up neatly, which is why people had to find creative ways to line them up anyway.

The first challenge was how to line up months to years, because the lunar and solar cycles don't actually line up. The early roman calendar as claimed to come from Romulus, but in actuality came from the Etruscans. It descended from calendars that tracked the lunar cycle, 29.5 day per month (alternating 29 and 30), but gradually changes were made to help line up the calendar to the solar year, much more useful for farming. There were ten months, followed by an uncertain number of days of winter until the new year would start.

50 years after Rome was founded, King Pompilius Numa attempted to realign the year with the calendar, by making the months add up to a year. However, the year only added up to 355 days, so periodically an intercalary period was needed to realign the calendar. The pontiff of the roman faith decided when and how long an intercalary should be, which eventually got abused to keep allies in office longer. This variability meant that in the provinces the exact date was often not known.

The abuse by the pontiffs led Julius Caesar to change this system to one we recognize now, except there was a leap year every 4 years, which caused the calendar to drift against the solar year. After Caesar's murder Mark Anthony renamed Quintilis to Iulius to honor him. Later Sextilis would be renamed to honor emperor Augustus.
If you hadn't noticed it yet, you should have noticed it by now. The rules by which we measure time, as passed to us from olden times, were decided by politicians for political reasons, which is why they're complete nonsense.

The Roman calendar measured years since the founding of Rome, Ab Urbe Condita or AUC. The AD calendar wasn't conceived until 525 AD, and popularized by Charlemagne around 800 AD. Dionysius Exiguus, the monk who conceived it, made some errors which cause Jesus to actually be born in 5 BC. It was the dark ages after all.
The AD system does not have a year zero, because it used roman numerals. Astronomers do use a year zero, putting it at 1 BC in the AD system.

Pope Gregory's calendar introduced the leap year rule we now know: Every year that is exactly divisible by four is a leap year, except for years that are exactly divisible by 100, but these centurial years are leap years if they are exactly divisible by 400.
This calendar took a long time to be adopted by countries. As a consequence, historical dates don't always mean what you think they do.
This calendar drifts one day per 7700 years, meaning in 1.4 million years winter falls in summer. Herschel's calendar fixes it by making multiples of 4000 not be leap years, which drifts only one day per 30.000 years.

Many cultures used weeks to track the phases of the moon, alternating 7 and 8 day weeks. The romans originally had an 8 day week, inherited from the Etruscans. However, for reasons which are not clear, the romans gradually moved to a 7 day week, with the days named according to the seven heavenly bodies visible with the naked eye. The germanic tribes didn't particularly care for those names, so they renamed most of them.

Hours don't come from the romans but from the ancient Egyptians. They divided the day into 10 hours and 2 twilight hours, then later the night hours were divided similarly. Also later (~200 BC) the Greek astronomer Eratosthenes would divide each hour into 60 minutes and each minute into 60 seconds. The duodecimal (base 12) system is a pre-arabic system of counting, easy to do because you count finger joints on your hand with your thumb. The sexagesimal (base 60) system is convenient for expressing fractions.

what is time, now?

As time marched on, it became more important to now precisely what time it was "now". And in order to do that, measuring time moved from being the province of politics to being the province of science.

SCIENCE!


"SI second"

The duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation corresponding to the transition between the two hyperfine levels of the ground state of the caesium-133 atom. (1967)
The length of a day is not a fixed duration. As the moon orbits the earth, it generates tides. These tides transfer momentum from the earth to the moon, causing the moon to recede by 4 cm per year, and causing the earth to slow down its rotation by a tiny amount, just enough to subtly alter the length of a day, and therefore the length of a second.
When this became a real problem for science, it was decided to standardize the second in terms of something that was not the movement of a heavenly body: the rotation of a caesium atom.

TAI

With a definition in hand, clocks could be made to measure the passing of time very precisely. Pictured here is the first generation of atomic beam clocks, built around the cesium atom in the 1950's. A group of around 450 cesium atomic clocks is ticking away in around 80 scientific laboratories around the world, and the weighted average of their measurement is known as International Atomic Time, aka Temps Atomique International, or TAI.

CUT TUC UTC

TAI + 10s + 27s

day = 86400s (+ 1)

However, since the length of a second is fixed, and the day is slowly growing longer, this means that the SI day is a tiny bit too short. For this reason, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) decides about 6 months in advance when to add a leap second, which adds one second to the latest minute of the day, creating a 61 second minute.
The result of adding leap seconds to TAI is UTC, coordinated universal time. The british wanted CUT, the french wanted TUC, and UTC is the compromise (actual reason for why that is the acronym). The leap second is added at midnight UTC, so it falls in different times in different time zones.
UTC started out in 1972 with a 10 second difference compared to TAI, on top of which 27 leap seconds were added.
Gotcha: it's not possible to predict time to the second more than a year in advance, so it is not known exactly when leap seconds are needed.
Because the irregular 61 second minutes introduced by leap seconds proved too complicated to handle in computer software, it was decided to phase out leap seconds by 2035.

A time zone identifier (e.g. "Europe/Brussels") determines a function or calculation rule. This function takes a time in UTC and outputs a time in the local time for that time zone. More accurately, it calculates the offset between UTC and local time at that instant in time, which can then be added to UTC to get the local time. The process is not reversible, you cannot derive UTC from local time without knowing the offset (the result of the prior calculation).
Regions not near the tropics tend to observe DST because it produces longer evenings. DST was first coined by Ben Franklin and adopted in Germany in 1916, mostly abandoned after WW2 due to cheap electrical lighting, and reintroduced during the energy crisis.

theory ≠ practice

  1. Coordinated Time
  2. Local Time (Zoned)
  3. Wall Clock Time
So, in theory, all we need to represent all time is universal coordinated time, to represent a particular moment in time, or zoned time, the same moment expressed in local time.
In practice however, people can set the clock on the wall to whatever they want, and sometimes a computer system needs to register that actual wall clock time, not whatever coordinated time that corresponded to. (e.g. time of death, start/end time of a room booking)

what is time, in computers?

System time

RTC - milliseconds - discontinuous

Date.now()

HPET - nanoseconds - monotonic

window.performance.now()

The Real Time Clock is kept in hardware, battery-backed, and allows the OS to get time with a resolution of seconds. IRQ8 is triggered several times a second with the latest value.
The RTC is backed by a quartz crystal, which oscillates at a predictable frequency when voltage is applied to it. The frequency varies with the temperature, which means this is not a precise or long-term reliable time source.
The high precision event timer counts nanoseconds since CPU reset, highly precise and monotonic. It is however not a clock, since you cannot obtain time from it.

Gotcha

VM clock drift

The RTC is semi-reliably in hardware, but becomes less so in virtualization. It can slow down a lot of the host is overburdened and cannot execute the guest OS at full speed. If you deploy NTP on both the host and guest, they can enter into duels, which gets ugly. It is generally considered best practice to install the guest tools to synchronize the guest clock with the host, and to run NTP only on the host.

The NTP algorithm continually adjusts the local clock to match the reference clock obtained from the time server. If the delta is > 128 ms it sets the local clock, otherwise it slews it, speeding it up or down, by 0.5 ms per second. The consequence is that you can't really know whether delta's you obtain from the clock are in any way reliable.
Because the NTP protocol relies on measuring latency to the NTP server, it is not very precise over the general internet. To work around this, data centers use other ways to update the time on their time servers.

GPS

~ 100 nanoseconds

Essentially the only reliable time source is an atomic clock. Data centers often have rubidium-based atomic clocks on-site to do precise timekeeping, but the problem becomes how to set them precisely to begin with. To do that, there is dedicated server hardware that gets its time from the GPS network. GPS satellites carry multiple rubidium atomic clocks, and are therefore able of communicating very precise time. Using GPS receivers you can get time accurate to ~100 ns.
Fun fact: GPS satellites are designed to run 38 micro-seconds per day too slow when on earth to compensate for the relativistic effects of the increased distance from the gravity mass of the earth (+45ms) and the relative speed (-7ms) of being in orbit.
Another fun fact: by combining GPS sync and atomic clocks Google's TrueTime guarantees that time across their network never deviates more than 7 ms, and their distributed database technology is built around this assumption.

Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie working on the C port of Unix in 1972.
1st edition unix was designed for the PDP-11/20, which had 24 KB of RAM, so it needed an extremely compact way of representing time.
The initial time representation of 1st ed. unix was linked to the rate of the system clock on the PDP-11. It had epoch at january 1st 1971 and could represent 2.5 years of time. The epoch was moved several times.
4th edition unix, ported to C, moved the epoch to 1st Jan. 1970 and counted seconds.

The more flexible and modern universal way of representing time is as an ISO 8601 date/time string. This gives us a lot of capability, representing coordinated time, local time, with as much precision as we want, and an optional time zone indicator that was used to calculate the offset, it does this at a price.
The first downside is that the string takes a much larger storage space, the second downside is that it is hard to do date/time arithmetic with, and that means libraries will always convert it to a different representation internally, and the last major downside is that there's a ton of valid and invalid variations of this format, and if you roll your own code to parse or generate it you are likely to get it wrong.







Birth dates are special!

While the temptation to use ISO strings for all date/time needs is always there, there is the curious case of birth dates. In particular, that some people don't have one. When creating a system that needs to be able to store birthdates for people from across the world, the circumstance of people who only have a birth month and year, or even only a birth year, must be taken into account. ISO notation cannot represent this in a valid way. (In practice many systems force entering these people's birth dates as january 1st, causing a disproportionate number of hospital patients around Brussels to be born on new year's.)

Java 1.0 made many mistakes, but one of the biggest was the Date class. In essence, it was an object-oriented wrapper around a 64 bit unix time. In practice, it sucked. Very quickly the Java team needed they had to provide something better, and eventually in Java 8 they got around to it and gave us the java.time API.
Regrettably though, JavaScript took some of its early design cues from Java, including its annoyingly bad Date class. Which is why dealing with date and time in JavaScript has always been a pain, just like it was in Java. Thankfully, support for Temporal is rolling out, the port of java.time to JavaScript. Safari is the one hold-out that still needs to ship it, and when that happens JavaScript will finally have a good way of handling date and time built-in. Until then, we need to use a library or a polyfill.

When needing to know the current time in a typical web application, the best place to get it from is the server, because you don't know what time and time zone settings the client has. If multiple servers are involved, you should try to get the time from a single source, and typically the database is the best place to get it from. Similarly time zone offset calculations are best all done in the same place, to avoid IANA timezone database mismatches between servers.
When building internationalized applications it is often a good idea to give the user the ability to change their time zone on their profile, and adjust all time-based code to work in that time zone.
For the various programming languages is listed here what the preferred strategy is to work with timezone-aware date/time representations, and a possible fallback in cases where the preferred is not an option.

Test of time

  • Inject now
  • Test the future in the past
  • Use interesting dates
You want to cover your date/time handling code with tests, but you don't want those tests to be brittle. Having a random element like now in those tests can cause brittle behavior, so it is best to design the code so that you can inject a different value of now for test purposes.
Then, with that ability, you can put now at some point in the past, so that you can write tests that deal with past, present and future entirely in the past. The advantage is that calendar rules never change in the past, so you can reliably bake the desired behavior into your tests.
Finally, try to not just test the golden path, but test interesting boundary conditions, like around the new year, or on a leap day. Don't sweat the small stuff like leap seconds and DST, they rarely lead to significant bugs anyway.
